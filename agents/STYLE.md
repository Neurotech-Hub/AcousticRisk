# STYLE.md

Models of scientific argument for drafting. `WRITER.md` governs prose and evidence rules; `REVIEWER.md` governs critique. This file says which published review to emulate for each section and how.

**Emulate reasoning architecture, not voice.** Extract argument structure, evidence integration, and calibration of uncertainty. Never reuse distinctive wording, metaphors, or sentence constructions, and never store copied passages as templates.

## Exemplars

Read the local PDF sections listed before drafting the corresponding manuscript section. Page numbers are PDF pages.

### 1. Magrath et al. — comparative synthesis (default model)

Magrath, Haff, Fallow & Radford (2015) Eavesdropping on heterospecific alarm calls: from mechanisms to consequences. *Biol Rev* 90:560–586. doi:10.1111/brv.12122  
Local: [`literature/Magrath 2014.pdf`](../literature/Magrath%202014.pdf) (the 2015 issue version; filename uses the online-first year). BibTeX key: `MagrathEtAl2015`.

| Study | Pages |
|---|---|
| IV(1) Unlearned responses: acoustic similarity, attention-capturing sounds, novelty, innate response | 11–14 |
| IV(2) Learned responses: geographic and temporal evidence, mechanisms of learning | 14–16 |
| V(2) Relevance and reliability of heterospecific alarm calls | 17–19 |

Extract:
- **Concept-first paragraphs.** Open with a proposition, not an author. Introduce a study because it tests, supports, qualifies, or contradicts that proposition. Group evidence by mechanism or question, not species.
- **Alternative mechanisms.** Separate acoustic similarity, intrinsic attention capture, innate recognition, and learning; name the observations that discriminate among them. A response is not proof of a recognition mechanism.
- **Functional transitions.** Move between topics through biological consequences (recognition → reliability → costs of responding → ecological consequences), not announcements.

Paragraph: proposition or open question → strongest diagnostic evidence → comparison (species, condition, experiment) → inference the comparison supports → remaining limitation or functional consequence.

Avoid: piling up taxonomic examples without saying what each adds.

### 2. Blumstein — theory and prediction

Blumstein (2006) The multipredator hypothesis and the evolutionary persistence of antipredator behavior. *Ethology* 112:209–217. doi:10.1111/j.1439-0310.2006.01209.x  
Local: [`literature/Blumstein 2006.pdf`](../literature/Blumstein%202006.pdf). BibTeX key: `Blumstein2006`.

| Study | Pages |
|---|---|
| Existing Hypotheses to Explain Persistence (including the linkage argument) | 1–4 |
| Testing the Multipredator Hypothesis | 4–5 |
| Broader Implications of the Multipredator Hypothesis | 5–6 |

Extract:
- **Problem → alternatives → limitation → synthesis.** State a specific puzzle, present competing explanations fairly, say what each explains and fails to explain, then synthesize.
- **Explicit predictions.** Turn explanations into observable outcomes that distinguish hypotheses; separate supporting observations from definitive tests.
- **Cross-level reasoning.** Keep evolutionary function, ecological context, behavior, and proximate mechanism distinct; evidence at one level is not proof at another.

Paragraph: phenomenon → candidate explanation → distinguishing prediction → supporting and contradictory evidence → unresolved limitation or better explanation.

Avoid: presenting plausible proximate mechanisms as established when evidence is indirect.

### 3. Watabe-Uchida et al. — mechanistic neuroscience

Watabe-Uchida, Eshel & Uchida (2017) Neural circuitry of reward prediction error. *Annu Rev Neurosci* 40:373–394. doi:10.1146/annurev-neuro-072116-031109  
Local: [`literature/Watabe-Uchida 2017.pdf`](../literature/Watabe-Uchida%202017.pdf). Style model only; not in `references.bib`.

| Study | Pages |
|---|---|
| Arithmetic of Dopamine Prediction Errors | 3–5 |
| Complexities in the Dopamine Signal | 6–8 |
| Electrophysiology of Inputs | 12–13 |
| Functional Studies: Causality of Inputs | 14–15 |

Extract:
- **Evidence hierarchy.** Distinguish descriptive activity, cell-type identification, circuit manipulation, and causal demonstration; state what each method establishes and why later experiments add stronger or different evidence.
- **Computation first.** Define the functional question before the neural structures; organize by problem, not region or method.
- **Calibrated inference.** Describe the observation, then interpret only as far as the experiment allows; name alternatives and open questions.

Paragraph: computational or mechanistic question → observation → what the method permits one to infer → alternative mechanisms or stronger causal evidence → what remains unresolved.

Avoid: cognitive metaphors, rhetorical openings, and treating a neural signal as a direct measure of a psychological construct.

## Paragraph architecture

Pick the sequence that fits the paragraph's job. Do not pad a paragraph to complete a template.

| Function | Sequence |
|---|---|
| Establish a phenomenon | Observation → evidence → boundary → significance |
| Compare studies | Shared question → convergent findings → discrepancy → explanation |
| Evaluate mechanisms | Competing mechanisms → discriminating predictions → evidence → unresolved distinction |
| Develop theory | Puzzle → existing explanations → limitations → synthesis |
| Interpret neural data | Measurement → result → warranted inference → alternative explanation |
| Establish a gap | What is known → what current methods cannot distinguish → testable question |
| Derive a prediction | Hypothesis → predicted observation → competing prediction → discriminating experiment |
