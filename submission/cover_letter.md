Dear Editor,

I am pleased to submit the review article "Interpreting Acoustic Risk: Integrating Field Ethology and Laboratory Neuroscience in Rodents" for consideration in *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*.

Two literatures ask how rodents respond to sounds that signal danger, and they have developed largely apart. Field studies of marmots, ground squirrels, prairie dogs, and chipmunks show how free-living animals use alarm-call structure, heterospecific calls, and caller reliability under realistic ecological costs. Laboratory studies in rats and mice map the auditory, amygdala, and midbrain pathways that turn sound into defense, but mostly with conditioned tones, noise, or looming sounds rather than natural calls. This review connects the two and offers a receiver-centered framework for deciding which acoustic findings translate between them.

The framework rests on three demands that any receiver faces: responding rapidly to salient acoustic events, learning what particular sounds predict, and selecting actions according to context and cost. From these follow three principles: acoustic structure constrains recognition while experience determines its significance; recognizing danger and deciding what to do about it are separable; and experience updates different components of responding on different timescales. The manuscript states what each principle predicts and what would challenge it, summarizes the distinguishing observations in a main-text table, and sets out explicit limits on cross-species inference, including that no laboratory circuit has yet been shown to mediate a natural alarm response in a wild sciurid. A compact organizing notation is offered as a heuristic, not a fitted model.

I believe the manuscript suits *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* because it integrates behavioral ecology, learning theory, and systems neuroscience around a shared biological question, and because its framework is designed to guide experiments in both laboratory and field.

The manuscript contains four figures, two main-text tables, and two supplementary tables. Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic) assisted with literature research, developing organizing principles, and grammatical editing, and the conceptual illustrations in Figures 1–4 were created with ChatGPT (OpenAI) image generation; both are disclosed in the declarations, and the figure tools are also named in each caption.

This manuscript has not been published and is not under consideration elsewhere. I am the sole author and declare no competing interests.
