# When Does a Sound Mean Danger?
## Neural and Behavioral Responses to Ecologically Relevant Sounds in Rodents

**Manuscript type:** Question-driven review (behavioral ecology with proximate mechanism)  
**Target journal:** *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology* (Review). Consider a pre-submission inquiry to the Editors-in-Chief on topic fit.  
**Central question:** How do rodents recognize, interpret, and adapt to biologically meaningful sounds, and what determines — functionally and mechanistically — whether those sounds continue to influence behavior?

## Scope and organizing principles

This review focuses on the **receiver's interpretation of acoustic stimuli**, not vocal production. Its conceptual progression is **recognition → assessment and response → updating over time (learning, habituation, temporal expectation)**. Each question is posed first in functional and evolutionary terms (why should a receiver respond, and when should it stop?) and then addressed with proximate evidence where it sharpens the functional answer.

- **Species:** Field-studied rodents (ground squirrels, marmots, prairie dogs, chipmunks, and other sciurids) and laboratory mice and rats are **co-primary**. Field systems supply natural signal use, caller identity, reliability, and response persistence; laboratory systems supply controlled stimuli, developmental manipulation, and mechanism. Wild-derived or field-experienced laboratory animals are noted where available.
- **Stimuli:** Conspecific alarm, distress, and social calls; heterospecific alarm calls; predator-associated sounds; synthetic calls, pure tones, noise, and acoustically controlled stimuli.
- **Outcomes:** Orienting, vigilance and time budgets, freezing, flight (including flight initiation distance), approach, autonomic/endocrine responses, neural activity (including dopamine), learning, habituation, and recovery.
- **Valence contrast:** Although the focus is danger, appetitive conspecific calls (e.g., rat 50-kHz vocalizations) are included deliberately as a contrast case: they show that natural, meaningful sounds can engage the same systems with opposite valence, which is necessary to interpret claims that a sound is perceived as threatening.
- **Exclusions:** Visual looming, olfaction, and multisensory threat paradigms are not review topics; mention only when necessary to delimit an acoustic finding. Do not expand into a general review of vocalization production or sensory fear circuits.
- **Interpretive rule:** Keep **acoustic salience**, **ecological significance**, **perceived threat**, **positive/negative valence**, and **behavioral deterrence** distinct. A dopamine transient or a startle reflex is not automatically evidence of sustained avoidance.
- **Evidence quality:** Mark whether a result is directly demonstrated, inferred mechanistically, or proposed as a testable hypothesis; distinguish naïve from laboratory-experienced and field-experienced animals.

## Functional framework (introduction)

Establish the theoretical backbone used throughout, so that each question has an explicit functional prediction:

- **Signal detection and error management ("smoke detector principle"):** When missing a real threat is far costlier than responding to a false alarm, receivers should respond readily to uncertain cues — and frequent false alarms are expected, not anomalous. Frames Q1, Q2, and Q5.
- **Threat-sensitive predator avoidance and the economics of escape:** Response intensity should scale with assessed risk and be traded off against the costs of fleeing, hiding, or ceasing foraging. Frames Q4.
- **Predation risk allocation hypothesis:** The temporal pattern of risk (brief, rare pulses versus frequent or continuous risk) should shape how much vigilance animals allocate to each alarm. Frames Q6 and Q7. No rodent study has implemented it with acoustic cues; present it as theory that motivates Q7.
- **Eavesdropping and relaxed selection:** Receivers can exploit heterospecific signals; antipredator responses may persist or decay in the absence of particular predators (multipredator hypothesis). Frames Q2. Eavesdropping is well documented in rodents; the multipredator hypothesis has no direct rodent acoustic test and serves as framing.

**Central claim:** Acoustic meaning is not a fixed property of a waveform. It emerges from an interaction among signal structure, receiver predispositions, prior experience, source identity and reliability, environmental state, and the temporal history of exposure.

## Part I — Recognition and ecological meaning

### Q1. What makes an acoustic stimulus biologically meaningful?

**Core issue:** Which acoustic dimensions carry information for the receiver? The evidence does not support a general rule that natural calls outperform synthetic ones: natural calls often produce stronger responses than matched controls, but simplified synthetic stimuli can preserve much of the effect or acquire meaning through experience. Naturalness is neither necessary nor sufficient.

- Compare natural calls with matched tones, white noise, frequency sweeps, and synthetic or manipulated vocalizations.
- Separate sound intensity, spectral structure, modulation, temporal envelope, novelty, and species-specific meaning.
- Consider which acoustic features could be reliable cues of risk. Marmot calls code urgency (call rate, nonlinear noise) rather than predator type; prairie dog calls carry enough information for predator-specific escape without implying lexical labels.
- Neural discrimination need not be call-specific: frequency-matched tones reproduce most amygdala differences between natural rat call types, so "natural" is not a single causal variable.
- Ask whether natural calls produce responses not explained by conventional acoustic features alone, and which features survive simplification.
- Note that significance can interact with physical properties (e.g., natural-call advantages present at high but not lower intensity).
- Use field call structure as the strongest natural evidence: within-call features and call types in Richardson's ground squirrels alter vigilance, and prairie dog alarm calls given to different predators reproduce predator-specific escape on playback.
- Caution against ranking relevance by signal source: chipmunks respond more strongly to titmouse mobbing calls than to hawk calls.
- **Anchor evidence:** Willuhn et al. (2014), comparing rat 50-kHz vocalizations with control sounds and measuring nucleus accumbens dopamine (valence contrast); Wöhr & Schwarting (2007), synthetic sequences that still induce approach; Schiavo et al. (2020), synthetic temporal statistics acquiring behavioral meaning; Blumstein & Armitage (1997), urgency coding in marmot alarm calls; Sloan et al. (2005) and Swan & Hare (2008), information in Richardson's ground squirrel call types and syllables; Kiriazis & Slobodchikoff (2006), predator-specific alarm calls in Gunnison's prairie dogs; Schmidt et al. (2008), chipmunk responses to titmouse calls.

**Synthesis question:** What constitutes a convincing acoustic control when claiming that a response reflects *meaning* rather than stimulus physics?

### Q2. Are responses to conspecific, heterospecific, and predator-associated sounds innate or learned?

**Core issue:** Does ecological relevance automatically imply innate recognition? The evidence fits neither side of a simple innate/learned dichotomy. Working model: **innate acoustic bias → developmental and associative calibration → ecological experience**.

- Compare developmentally naïve, predator-naïve, laboratory-experienced, and wild/field-experienced rodents.
- Use two worked cases: predator calls (conflicting evidence in predator-naïve laboratory rodents; strong responses in field-experienced animals) and rat 22-kHz calls (selective freezing depends heavily on prior aversive experience; the 22-kHz frequency range may carry some innate salience; the strict self-vocalization "autoconditioning" account has been rejected).
- Present predator-call recognition in naïve laboratory rodents as an unresolved conflict: an inbred mouse strain responded selectively to owl calls (Hendrie & Neill 1991), whereas naïve mice, rats, and gerbils did not (Kindermann et al. 2009). Species, strain, and paradigm differences are the likely explanation; neither result settles the question.
- Contrast conspecific alarms and distress calls with heterospecific cues (eavesdropping) and vocalizations of predators. Rodents eavesdrop on bird alarms and on other rodents' alarms, and also use heterospecific *safety* cues (ordinary bird chatter). Gray squirrel evidence suggests both learned familiarity and acoustic generalization contribute.
- Examine individual learning, social learning, and the environmental experience required for threat recognition.
- Functional framing: when should selection favor innate recognition versus learning (predictability of predator community, cost of a first mistake)? Consider persistence or loss of recognition under relaxed predation.
- **Anchor evidence:** Hendrie & Neill (1991) and Kindermann, Siemers & Fendt (2009), conflicting results on avian predator-call recognition in predator-naïve laboratory rodents; Kim et al. (2010) and Calub et al. (2018), experience-dependent responses to rat 22-kHz calls; Mateo (1996), ontogeny of alarm-call discrimination in Belding's ground squirrels; Endres et al. (2007), frequency-range sensitivity versus learned call meaning in rats; Shriner (1998), heterospecific alarm-call responses in marmots and ground squirrels; Getschow et al. (2013), familiarity and acoustic generalization in gray squirrel eavesdropping; Lilly et al. (2019), heterospecific safety cues; Magrath et al. (2015) on heterospecific eavesdropping; Blumstein (2006) on the multipredator hypothesis.

**Synthesis question:** When does a sound carry intrinsic behavioral significance, and when is its significance acquired?

## Part II — Assessment and behavioral response

### Q3. What can neural measures reveal about how receivers assess a sound?

**Core issue:** Neural activation is evidence about proximate assessment, not a direct readout of perceived threat or of the eventual behavioral decision.

*Keep this section deliberately short; it serves the functional argument rather than surveying circuits.*

- Use one or two well-defined contrasts rather than a circuit atlas: appetitive conspecific calls versus aversive noise; nucleus accumbens versus tail-of-striatum dopamine responses to the same acoustic event.
- Distinguish stimulus novelty, prediction error, aversive value, arousal, and action selection, and state which of these a given measure can and cannot discriminate.
- Frame the section with four separable levels: acoustic detection, biological interpretation, action selection, and updating. Neural measures can dissociate from behavior at each step.
- Neural discrimination can occur without overt behavior: naïve rats show different amygdala firing to 22-kHz and 50-kHz calls without freezing, though matched tones reproduce most of this difference (a detection-level code). Spiny mice mount endocrine but not behavioral responses to owl calls. Absence of a behavioral response is not absence of assessment.
- Do not equate threat with more activation: in Richardson's ground squirrels, chirp playback *reduced* c-Fos in medial amygdala and superior colliculus. Whether accumbens dopamine scales with aversive noise intensity depends on preparation (yes in head-fixed mice, no in freely moving rats).
- Link back to function: which neural signatures would be expected if receivers were tracking risk, reliability, or timing (Q5–Q7)?
- Name the gap: most dopamine work uses noise, appetitive 50-kHz calls, or non-acoustic aversive stimuli; direct evidence for ecological threat calls (alarm or predator calls) is sparse.
- **Anchor evidence:** Willuhn et al. (2014), dopamine responses to appetitive rat ultrasonic vocalizations; Furuyama et al. (2026), region-specific dopamine responses to aversive noise; Goedhoop et al. (2022), accumbens dopamine tracking aversive duration and prediction; Parsana et al. (2012), amygdala discrimination of call valence without freezing; Freeman et al. (2020), call-specific c-Fos in a field sciurid.

**Synthesis question:** What exactly does a neural response tell us about what the animal inferred from the sound?

### Q4. What determines whether a sound evokes orienting, vigilance, freezing, flight, or approach?

**Core issue:** A sound's ecological effect depends on the behavioral response it elicits, and each response carries different costs and benefits.

- Distinguish rapid acoustic startle from orienting, assessment, defensive freezing, escape, and approach.
- Apply threat-sensitive avoidance and economics-of-escape predictions: response type and intensity should depend on assessed risk, distance to refuge, body condition, foraging opportunity, and group context.
- Consider acoustic features, prior experience, behavioral state, habitat, and perceived escape options.
- Include field measures (vigilance time budgets, flight initiation distance, time to resume foraging) alongside laboratory assays, and autonomic/endocrine measures when they help interpret ambiguous behavior.
- The same stimulus can produce different outputs across species and individuals (e.g., some rodents freeze to owl calls, others flee, others show endocrine stress with little overt behavior), so absence of freezing is not absence of a response. Argue for a multidimensional outcome set: short-latency orienting or startle, locomotion, approach or avoidance, vigilance, autonomic measures, and recovery latency.
- Field studies supply the costs that laboratory assays lack: alarm calls delay re-emergence and reduce food intake. In gray squirrels, predator-call playback suppressed and delayed foraging without changing distance to refuge.
- **Anchor evidence:** Mongeau et al. (2003), a standardized 17–20 kHz sweep evoking flight or freezing depending on cage familiarity and prior shock; Kiriazis & Slobodchikoff (2006), different escape actions to different prairie dog alarm-call categories; Baack & Switzer (2000), foraging costs of alarm calls in chipmunks; Jayne et al. (2015), acoustic risk and foraging in gray squirrels; Mateo (2010), predator-specific physiological responses to Belding's ground squirrel alarm calls; Hare (1998), graded vigilance, posture, and escape responses in Richardson's ground squirrels; Ydenberg & Dill (1986); Helfman (1989); Dill & Houtman (1989), refuge distance and flight in gray squirrels (visual cue; introduction only).

**Synthesis question:** When are laboratory responses valid proxies for meaningful ecological decisions?

## Part III — Updating responses over time

Q5 concerns **what the receiver learns about the signal** (information and reliability). Q6 concerns **how responsiveness changes with repetition and rest** (habituation dynamics). Q7 concerns **how the timing of sounds shapes expectation**. Keep these accounts separate; each section should state how its mechanism would be distinguished experimentally from the others.

### Q5. How do experience and signal reliability change the perceived meaning of a sound?

**Core issue:** Animals may learn not merely that a sound predicts danger, but how much the signal can be trusted.

- Cover associative learning, conditioned threat responses, social experience, and individual caller recognition.
- Examine how repeated false alarms, caller identity, and environmental context alter responsiveness.
- Functional framing: under error management, when should receivers discount an unreliable caller, and when should they keep responding despite false alarms?
- Diagnostic criterion: reliability learning predicts caller- or context-specific decline and selective renewal (e.g., when the caller changes); generic habituation does not.
- Separate identity from reliability. Caller identity is discriminated in sciurids, laboratory rats (stranger versus cagemate 50-kHz calls), and prairie voles (partner versus stranger). Reliability learning from acoustic signals is shown only in sciurids; comparable experiments are lacking in laboratory rats and mice, and these literatures have rarely been integrated.
- Reliability effects differ in direction: juvenile ground squirrels discount unreliable callers, whereas marmots respond *more* to them, apparently investing in independent assessment. Ask when ignoring versus checking unreliable information should be favored. Caller familiarity had no effect in marmots.
- **Anchor evidence:** Hare & Atkins (2001), juvenile Richardson's ground squirrels reduce responses to callers whose alarms are repeatedly unpaired with a predator (published in *Behav Ecol Sociobiol*); Blumstein et al. (2004), marmots respond more to unreliable callers and also weigh distance and number of callers; Vielle et al. (2021) and Warren et al. (2025), caller identity in laboratory rats and prairie voles; Hare (1998), caller-specific habituation and renewal of vigilance; Blumstein & Daniel (2004), caller discrimination in marmots; Hare & Warkentin (2012), no preference for the mother's calls, suggesting reliability or familiarity rather than kinship.

**Synthesis question:** Can a decline in response reflect learned unreliability rather than generic sensory habituation?

### Q6. Does ecological significance change the rate of habituation or recovery?

**Core issue:** Biologically meaningful sounds may have different response trajectories over repeated exposures, but may still habituate.

- A falling response can reflect at least four processes that produce similar curves: **sensory habituation**, **learned unreliability** (Q5), **reduced motivational significance**, and **active prediction** (Q7). State which designs can tell them apart.
- Distinguish within-session habituation, between-session retention, spontaneous recovery, stimulus generalization, and dishabituation, using the formal criteria of Rankin et al. (2009) to separate habituation from fatigue, sensory adaptation, and the other processes above.
- Compare natural calls with artificial approximations under matched intensity and temporal schedules.
- Discuss timescales (seconds, hours, days) and whether stimulus identity affects recovery.
- Functional framing: higher-cost misses should favor slower habituation or faster recovery for sounds that are more likely to signal real risk.
- Separate two claims. Field evidence for stimulus-specific habituation and identity-specific recovery is strong (ground squirrels, marmots). Evidence that ecological significance itself changes the *rate* or time course of habituation, relative to an acoustically matched control, is thin and rests mainly on Evans & Hammond (1983); modern replication with neural measures is a clear opportunity. Significance does not guarantee persistence: rat approach to prosocial 50-kHz calls habituates even when playback predicts social interaction (Berz et al. 2022).
- **Anchor evidence:** Evans & Hammond (1983), natural rat distress squeals versus synthetic approximations, with greater recovery after rest for the natural call at high intensity, and a companion paper on context generalization of habituation; Hare (1998), caller-specific dishabituation in free-living ground squirrels; Blumstein & Daniel (2004), habituation and recovery to caller identity in marmots; Rankin et al. (2009), general principles of habituation.

**Synthesis question:** Do ecologically significant sounds retain responsiveness, or simply recover differently after rest?

### Q7. How does temporal predictability influence neural and behavioral responses to repeated sounds?

**Core issue:** The statistics of playback can change expectation independently of acoustic identity.

- Lead with the predation risk allocation hypothesis: how the frequency and predictability of risky periods should shape vigilance allocated to each alarm.
- Compare fixed intervals, variable intervals, random schedules, and structured environmental timing.
- Introduce temporal expectation and conditional event probability (*hazard*) as conceptual tools, not proven optimization rules.
- Separate interval length from unpredictability; account for recovery time and overall stimulus exposure.
- Consider whether biologically meaningful signals interact differently with temporal prediction than neutral tones or noise.
- Natural temporal structure is decoded, but not uniformly: repetition and call rate affect vigilance in Richardson's ground squirrels, yet adults ignore rate changes in juvenile calls that juveniles themselves use as an urgency cue.
- Keep the empirical conclusion narrow: regular timing can promote habituation, and variable timing can preserve responsiveness in some paradigms. Field work has examined call rate, repetition, sequence, identity, and reliability, but no study has compared interval distributions (fixed, uniform, Gaussian, exponential, heavy-tailed) using natural rodent calls; constant-hazard timing is a hypothesis, not a result. Present Q7 as a genuine open question.
- **Anchor evidence:** Lima & Bednekoff (1999), predation risk allocation; Sloan et al. (2005) and Sloan & Hare (2006), temporal structure and age-dependent rate sensitivity in Richardson's ground squirrel calls; Davis (1970), regular versus variable intervals in rodent startle habituation; Herry et al. (2007), temporal jitter of neutral tone pulses preventing rapid mouse amygdala habituation (variability, not distribution shape, was manipulated); Goedhoop et al. (2022), prediction altering accumbens dopamine recovery after noise.

**Synthesis question:** Which temporal statistics preserve behavioral responsiveness, and do any generalize from artificial laboratory sounds to natural calls?

## Integrative synthesis — From perception to response persistence

Bring the seven questions into a common receiver-centered model, with functional costs (missed threat versus false alarm) as the currency that links its stages:

1. **Stimulus:** Acoustic structure, naturalness, call type, source identity.
2. **Prior beliefs:** Innate predisposition, development, prior exposure, social learning, ecological context.
3. **Assessment:** Salience, value, prediction, uncertainty, and candidate proximate mechanisms.
4. **Behavioral output:** Orienting, vigilance, freezing, escape, approach, or no overt response, weighed against their costs.
5. **Updating:** Learning about signal reliability, habituation, recovery, and temporal expectation.
6. **Feedback:** Updated expectations modify responses to subsequent sounds.

### Critical distinctions to preserve throughout

| Distinction | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Natural versus artificial sound | Tests ecological significance, but can be confounded by acoustic differences. |
| Conspecific alarm versus predator vocalization | Similar behavioral consequences need not share recognition mechanisms. |
| Naïve versus field-experienced receiver | Recognition of threat may require experience. |
| Laboratory versus field context | Costs of responding (lost foraging, exposure) are often absent in the laboratory. |
| Acoustic startle versus defensive avoidance | Strong short-latency reflexes need not predict long-term deterrence. |
| Dopamine response versus behavioral meaning | Dopamine signals depend on region, timing, context, and valence. |
| Acoustic detection versus biological interpretation versus action selection | Neural and physiological responses can separate sounds without any behavioral change, and matched tones can reproduce "call-specific" neural codes. |
| Caller identity versus caller reliability | Identity discrimination is widespread across rodents; reliability learning is shown only in sciurids. |
| Habituation versus learned unreliability | Both reduce response but imply different mechanisms and interventions. |
| Unpredictable timing versus longer recovery | Must be dissociated experimentally. |

## Conclusion and future directions

### From acoustic perception to predictive models of behavioral responsiveness

The review should conclude by identifying which mechanisms plausibly support predictive models of responses to repeated sound:

- **Stimulus identity and ecological relevance** — which calls are meaningful to which receivers?
- **Learning and reliability** — how does a signal's history update its interpretation?
- **Temporal expectation** — what properties of stimulus timing are predictable or surprising, and how should risk allocation shape responses?
- **Habituation and recovery** — how does responsiveness evolve after repetition and silence?
- **Ecological outcome** — which neural or laboratory measures predict sustained vigilance or avoidance in the field?

Propose rigorous, **testable hypotheses** that pair laboratory and field systems (e.g., testing reliability and timing predictions derived from sciurid field data in controlled laboratory paradigms, and vice versa). Applied playback scheduling can be mentioned briefly as one domain where these hypotheses matter: comparative timing policies (fixed, uniformly jittered, or hazard-based schedules) tested under matched acoustic exposure. This is a future-direction example, not a product-centric argument or an efficacy claim. Keep it short, and declare any related competing interests in the disclosure statement.

## Positioning and novelty

This is **not** a general review of rodent vocalization production, a survey of all sensory fear pathways, or a review of wildlife-deterrent devices. Its contribution is a **question-driven synthesis** of how the *receiver* assigns meaning to sound and how that meaning changes with experience and time, framed by functional theory of antipredator behavior and informed by proximate mechanism.

The central bridge is between sciurid field ecology (natural signal use, caller identity, threat discrimination, reliability, and response persistence) and mouse/rat neuroscience (controlled stimuli, recordings, manipulations). Evidence from one should motivate hypotheses for the other, without treating cross-species generalizations as established findings.
